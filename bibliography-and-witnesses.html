A map of the scholarship

One poem, twelve centuries of witnesses

There is no autograph of the poem. What we have instead is a continuous chain of witnesses — a text copied, selected, printed, annotated and collated by many hands — and, alongside it, an unbroken scholarly tradition from 1913 to the present. The two lists that follow are that chain: Primary (every surviving edition, imprint and print) and Secondary (every study of the poem). This is the "map of the scholarship" you asked to see before discussing technique.

The history of the text, in brief. Li Bai left no manuscript in his own hand; the poem reaches us only through witnesses. A Tang scribe in Dunhuang copied it under the title Gu Shu Dao Nan; in 753 an anthologist printed it in a slightly regularised form; Song and Yuan printers carried it into print while preserving older readings in their collation notes; and the Quan Tang Shi of 1706 fixed the version that all subsequent editions — and all English translations — reproduce. Modern collations and recent studies have since mapped exactly where those witnesses diverge. It is this history — the manuscript tradition against the print tradition, and the many hands of scribes, anthologists, editors and printers — that makes the poem, as you observed, so well suited to a digital scholarly edition.

The dating debate

The dating of the poem is part of the textual problem. Two scholarly positions, to be recorded in the edition as attributed hypotheses (@cert / @resp):

742–744 — written during Li Bai's first stay in Chang'an (Yu Xianhao; An Qi). Supported by the He Zizhang anecdote (Meng Qi, Benshi shi).
745–746 — written around Li Bai's second stay / departure from Chang'an (Zhan Ying's later view and others). Supported by internal allusions and the poem's addressee question.

The Four Witnesses, in Their Own Words

Primary · 1

敦煌写本 P.2567（+P.2552）《古蜀道难》

c. 8th century
法藏敦煌唐诗选抄残卷，卷中此诗题作《古蜀道难》。现存最早《蜀道难》写本，无名抄手所抄，与通行本有 12 处文辞差异。
A Tang anthology fragment among the Dunhuang manuscripts (Pelliot chinois), Bibliothèque nationale de France. The earliest extant witness of the poem — copied by an anonymous scribe, and titled there Gu Shu Dao Nan ("The Old 'Hard Roads of Shu'"). It differs from the received text in 12 places.
Dunhuang original textWorking English translation
噫吁嚱，危乎高哉！Alas! How steep, how high!
蜀道之難，難於上青天！The road to Shu is hard — harder than climbing the blue sky.
蠶叢及魚鳧，開國何茫然！Cancong and Yufu — how dim and remote their founding of the realm!
爾來四萬八千歲，乃不與秦塞通人煙。Since then forty-eight thousand years — and never once a path between Qin's frontier and human smoke.
西當太白有鳥道，可以橫絕峨眉巔。West, on Taibai's peak, only a bird's path — and that could cut across to Emei's summit.
蛇崩山摧壯士死，然後天梯石棧方鉤連。The serpent broke, the mountain crashed, the strong men died — only then were sky-ladders and plank-paths joined.
上有橫河斷海之浮雲，下有銜波逆折之回川。Above, floating clouds that dam the rivers and sever the seas; below, waves that clasp and coil, eddying back in the turning stream.
黃鶴之飛尚不得過，猿猱欲度愁攀牽。Even the yellow crane in flight cannot cross; monkeys that would pass grieve at the clinging pull of the rocks.
青泥何盤盤，百步九折縈巖巒。How Qingni coils and coils — a hundred paces, nine turns, winding round the crags.
捫參歷井仰脅息，以手撫心坐長嘆。Touching the stars of Shen, crossing Jing, gasping toward heaven; hand pressed to heart, seated in a long sigh.
問君西遊何時還？畏途巉巖不可攀。I ask you, journeying west — when will you return? The dreaded road, sheer cliffs that none may climb.
但見悲鳥號古木，雄飛從雌繞花間。Mournful birds cry in ancient trees: the male flies, the female follows him, circling among the blossoms.
又聞子規啼月，愁空山。And the cuckoo's cry at the moon — sorrow in the empty hills.
蜀道之難，難於上青天，使人聽此凋朱顏！The road to Shu is hard, harder than the blue sky — hearing it, the rose drains from the cheek.
連峰入煙幾千尺，枯松倒挂倚絕壁。Linked peaks sink into the mist, some thousand feet; withered pines hang inverted from sheer walls.
飛湍瀑流爭喧豗，砯崖轉石萬壑雷。Rushing rapids and cataracts vie in roar; pounding the cliffs, rolling stones — thunder through ten thousand ravines.
其險若此，嗟爾遠道之人胡為乎來哉！Such is the peril! Why, traveller from afar, have you come at all?
劍閣崢嶸而崔嵬，一夫當關，萬夫莫開。The Sword-Gate towers, rugged and vast: one man holds the pass, ten thousand cannot breach it.
所守或匪親，化為狼與豺。If its keeper is no trusted kin, he turns to wolf and jackal.
朝避猛虎，夕避長蛇，磨牙吮血，殺人如麻。At dawn flee the fierce tiger, at dusk shun the long serpent — grinding teeth, sucking blood, slaying men like hemp.
（無「錦城雖云樂」一聯）蜀道之難，難於上青天，側身西望令人嗟！(No couplet on Jincheng here — the warning "return home early" does not yet exist.) The road to Shu is hard, harder than the blue sky — I turn, gaze west, and one sigh rises.
Transcription reconstructed from the collation list in Luo Jianxin, Guangming Daily, 16 June 2025 (12 variant points, each verified against the received text); manuscript images: BnF Pelliot chinois 2567 + 2552, also via IDP (International Dunhuang Project). The variant 蛇／虵 and popular-character forms of the scroll are noted in Luo's study.

殷璠《河岳英灵集》卷上

753 · earliest anthology
现存最早收录此诗的选本（天宝十二载编成），殷璠并评曰「奇之又奇，自骚人以还，鲜有此体调」。相较敦煌本有 9 处修辞调整，已初见「规范化」意识。
The earliest anthology to include the poem, compiled by Yin Fan in 753 — with the first piece of criticism ever attached to it: "marvellous upon marvellous; since the Sao poets, rarely has such a register appeared." Compared with the Dunhuang witness it makes 9 rhetorical adjustments: the first visible gesture of editorial normalisation.

宋蜀刻本《李太白文集》卷三（南宋初眉山刻）

c. 1127–1163
宋蜀刻本翻刻自元丰三年（1080）晏知止苏州本，双行小注中保留大量「一作」异文——注意红字小注：敦煌本的读法在这里被宋本校注原样保存。今国图藏残本（配缪曰芑本）、日本静嘉堂文库藏足本。
The Song printed edition from Meishan, Sichuan, itself a reprint of the 1080 Suzhou edition. Its interlinear collation notes ("one text reads…", shown in cinnabar below) preserve several Dunhuang-era readings inside the printed apparatus — the transmissional layers, already visible in the Song edition itself. Surviving copies: National Library of China (fragment) and the Seikadō Bunko, Japan (complete).

通行本（《全唐诗》卷一六二系统）

received text
明清以降的「定本」，一切通行选本与一切英译者所依据的底本。与敦煌本对读，可见十二处差异如何塑造了英文读者收到的《蜀道难》。
The received text — the "definitive" version stabilised in Ming–Qing printings (here following Quan Tang Shi, juan 162). This is the base text used by every English translator: what the English reader receives is this witness, not the others. Read its cinnabar marks against Primary 1 and the twelve divergences appear at a glance.

又闻子规啼夜月，愁空山。And the cuckoo crying in the night moon — sorrow in the empty hills.
蜀道之难，难于上青天，使人听此凋朱颜！连峰去天不盈尺，枯松倒挂倚绝壁。Hearing it, the rose drains from the cheek. Linked peaks stand less than a foot from the sky; withered pines hang inverted from sheer walls.
飞湍瀑流争喧豗，砯崖转石万壑雷。其险也如此，嗟尔远道之人胡为乎来哉！Rushing rapids and cataracts vie in roar; pounding cliffs roll stones — thunder in ten thousand ravines. Such, indeed, is the peril! Why, traveller from afar, have you come at all?
剑阁峥嵘而崔嵬，一夫当关，万夫莫开。所守或匪亲，化为狼与豺。The Sword-Gate towers: one man holds the pass, ten thousand cannot breach it. If its keeper is no trusted kin, he turns to wolf and jackal.
朝避猛虎，夕避长蛇。磨牙吮血，杀人如麻。锦城虽云乐，不如早还家。At dawn flee the tiger, at dusk shun the serpent — grinding teeth, sucking blood, slaying men like hemp. Though Jincheng is merry, better to return home early — the couplet every English reader knows, unknown to the Dunhuang scribe.
蜀道之难，难于上青天，侧身西望长咨嗟！The road to Shu is hard, harder than the blue sky — I turn, gaze west, and heave a long, deep sigh.
Following Quan Tang Shi, juan 162 (Wikisource transcription; the poem is in the public domain). Every English translation of the poem — Obata 1922 onward — is made from a text of this lineage. That observation is the seed of the thesis: the English reader receives the result of a transmission that this edition will make visible.
